# WEEK 11 --- SMART GRID, IIoT, AND BIG DATA ANALYTICS

## 1. Smart Grid and Modern Electrical Networks

### 1.1 What is a Smart Grid?
A **Smart Grid** is a modern electrical power grid enhanced with digital communication, sensing, and automated control technologies. 

```
Traditional Grid vs. Smart Grid:
Feature                   Traditional Grid             Smart Grid
Energy Flow               One-way (Power plant → User) Bidirectional (Two-way)
Communication             One-way or manual            Two-way real-time communication
Monitoring                Manual / slow inspection     Automated continuous digital sensing
Restoration               Manual field repair crews    Self-healing / automatic rerouting
Generation                Centralized only             Centralized + Distributed (Solar, Wind)
```

**Key exam point:** The primary distinguishing feature of a smart grid is **bidirectional flows of energy with two-way communication and control**.

### 1.2 Phasor Measurement Units (PMU) & SCADA
- **Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU):** A high-speed digital device placed across power transmission systems to sample electrical waves (voltage and current phasors) with microsecond GPS time-synchronization.
- **Role of SCADA:** Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems gather data from PMUs and field devices to provide wide-area grid visibility and automated emergency control.

## 2. Smart Metering, HEMS, and Energy Pricing

### 2.1 Smart Meter
A **Smart Meter** is an electronic device that digitally records electricity consumption and communicates the information back to the utility service provider for monitoring and billing.
- In a smart home, the smart meter provides the **interface between the consumer and the energy service provider**.

### 2.2 Time-of-Use (ToU) Pricing
- Electricity demand varies throughout the day. **Time-of-Use (ToU)** pricing charges different electricity rates depending on the time of day:
  - **On-Peak Hours:** High demand periods with high electricity prices.
  - **Off-Peak Hours:** Low demand periods (e.g., night) with lower electricity prices.
- **Consumer benefit:** Consumers are encouraged to operate heavy appliances (washing machines, EV charging, pumps) during **off-peak hours** to save costs and reduce grid strain.

### 2.3 Net Metering
- **Net Metering** is a billing mechanism that credits solar energy system owners for the electricity they add to the grid.
- **The two energy flows:**
  1. **Energy inflow:** Electricity consumed by the customer from the grid.
  2. **Energy outflow:** Excess electricity generated by the customer (e.g. rooftop solar panels) supplied back into the grid.
- Final billing is based on **net electricity consumed**: $\text{Bill} = \text{Energy Inflow} - \text{Energy Outflow}$.

## 3. Distribution Intelligence and Smart Grid Security

### 3.1 Distribution Intelligence
- Distribution intelligence uses sensors, smart meters, and automated switches on electricity feeder lines.
- **Core functions:**
  - Detecting the exact source and location of power outages.
  - Supporting **automatic power-flow restoration** (isolating faults and switching lines without human delay).
  - Optimizing voltage levels and power quality.

### 3.2 Gateways and Data Aggregator Units (DAUs)
- In smart grid communication architecture:
  - Individual smart meters send data locally to an **IoT Gateway**.
  - The Gateway connects closely located smart meters to **Data Aggregator Units (DAUs)**.
  - DAUs aggregate regional meter readings and forward them to utility control centers.

### 3.3 Security Attacks in Smart Grids
- **Denial of Service (DoS) Attack:** An attack aimed at making smart grid systems or information inaccessible to legitimate operators when needed. DoS attacks specifically compromise the **Availability** of smart grid components.

## 4. Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)

### 4.1 What is IIoT?
The **Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)** is the application of IoT technologies in manufacturing, utilities, and heavy industry. It connects industrial machinery, sensor networks, and automation systems directly with enterprise planning, logistics, and product lifecycle management.

### 4.2 Migration Strategy: Wrap and Re-use
- Replacing entire factory floors of machinery is prohibitively expensive ("Rip and Replace").
- **Wrap and Re-use:** An engineering approach where modern IoT sensors, microcontrollers, and communication interfaces are wrapped around existing legacy industrial machinery, allowing companies to gain smart capabilities without discarding functional equipment.

### 4.3 Mining Applications with WSN
- Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) inside underground mines monitor critical environmental parameters:
  - Air quality and oxygen ($O_2$) levels.
  - Poisonous gases (carbon monoxide $CO$, methane $CH_4$, hydrogen sulfide $H_2S$).
  - Temperature, humidity, and seismic rock stability for early worker evacuation.

## 5. Big Data and Pre-processing in IoT

### 5.1 The 5Vs of Big Data
IoT systems generate massive streams of data characterized by the 5Vs:
- **Volume:** Massive scale of data generated by billions of sensors.
- **Velocity:** High speed at which new data streams arrive.
- **Variety:** Heterogeneous data formats (text, time-series, audio, video).
- **Veracity:** The truthfulness, trustworthiness, bias, and noise in collected data.
- **Value:** Transforming raw sensor measurements into useful business insights.

### 5.2 Redundancy Mitigation
- IoT sensors often report identical readings repeatedly (e.g., room temperature staying 25°C).
- **Redundancy mitigation** eliminates repeated or unnecessary data through **detection, filtering, and compression** before transmission or storage to conserve bandwidth and energy.

### 5.3 Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS)
- **HDFS Architecture:**
  - **Namenode:** Manages filesystem metadata (directory tree, file permissions, block locations). Does NOT store actual data files.
  - **Datanode:** Stores the actual file data blocks on disk across distributed cluster machines.

1. Smart Grid – Part I
Main focus: Transforming the traditional electrical grid. Based on the first section of the PDF.
1.1 Traditional Electrical Grid
A traditional electrical grid is the system used to generate electricity at power plants and deliver it to homes, offices, and industries.
Main features of a traditional grid
- Electricity is commonly generated at centralized power plants.
- Electricity normally flows in one direction: from the power plant through the transmission and distribution system to the consumer.
- Monitoring, fault detection, and restoration can require manual work.
- There is limited communication between the electricity provider and consumers.
Example: Electricity is generated at a power plant, carried through power lines, and supplied to a house. A traditional meter may need to be read manually by a meter reader.
1.2 What Is a Smart Grid?
A Smart Grid is a modern electricity network that uses information technology, sensors, digital meters, communication, and control systems to deliver electricity efficiently, reliably, and securely.

The lecture also calls a Smart Grid:
- Electricity with a brain
- The energy internet
- The electronet
The lecture presents the NIST idea of a Smart Grid as a modernized grid that supports two-way communication and bidirectional energy flow, enabling new functions and applications.
How a Smart Grid works
1. Smart meters and sensors collect information about electricity use and grid conditions.
2. Communication networks send this information to the electricity provider or control centre.
3. Software analyses the information.
4. The system can help operators make better decisions, control equipment, and respond to faults.
5. Consumers can also receive information about their usage and pricing.
Easy example: A smart meter automatically reports your electricity use. The electricity provider can use the information to understand demand, while you can use it to identify when your home consumes the most power.
1.3 Benefits of a Smart Grid
Benefits for consumers
- Real-time usage information: Consumers can see how much electricity they are using.
- Potentially lower bills: Appliances can be scheduled for lower-cost hours when time-based pricing is available.
- Support for smart devices: Smart appliances and electric vehicles can coordinate their electricity use with the grid.
- More pricing choices: Consumers may be offered different pricing plans.
- Better visibility: Consumers can better understand their energy consumption patterns.
Benefits for electricity providers and other stakeholders
- Higher reliability: Better monitoring can help identify problems.
- Fewer blackouts and brownouts: Improved control can reduce the frequency or impact of disturbances.
- Better analysis and decisions: The system can collect information for monitoring and planning.
- More efficient delivery: The provider can manage grid resources more effectively.
- Renewable energy integration: Solar and wind resources can be coordinated with the main grid.
- Improved management of distributed resources: Batteries, microgrids, and local power-generation resources can be better managed.
- Potentially lower operating costs: Automation and better planning may reduce operational and management costs.
1.4 Properties of a Smart Grid
1. Consumer participation
Consumers receive information and can take part in managing their electricity use. They may monitor consumption, control smart appliances, or use building automation systems.
2. Real-time pricing
Electricity prices may change according to the time of day or demand. This can encourage consumers to shift flexible activities to less busy hours.
3. Distributed generation
Electricity can be generated near the place where it is used. Rooftop solar panels are a common example. The Smart Grid can help coordinate these sources with the main grid.
4. Power-system efficiency
Monitoring, asset management, optimal equipment use, distribution automation, and protection can improve how the power system operates.
5. Power quality
Power quality concerns whether electricity is supplied in a stable and suitable form. Smart Grid systems may support:
- Self-healing responses to certain faults.
- Frequency monitoring and control.
- Load forecasting.
- Early identification of disturbances.
1.5 Smart Grid Architecture
Smart Grid architecture connects homes, meters, local communication networks, data aggregators, wide-area communication, control systems, and the electricity network.

A simplified information path is:
Home appliances / sensors
          ↓
     Smart meter
          ↓
      Gateway
          ↓
Data Aggregator Unit (DAU)
          ↓
Meter Data Management System (MDMS) / control centre
          ↓
Monitoring, analysis, pricing, and grid control
Key architecture parts
- Smart meter: Measures electricity use and exchanges information.
- Gateway: Connects local meters and devices to a wider network.
- Home Area Network (HAN): Connects devices within a home or building.
- Neighborhood Area Network (NAN): Connects devices and meters across a local area.
- Wide Area Network (WAN): Connects systems over a larger geographic area.

- MDMS: Manages meter data and helps coordinate smart-grid communication.
- Operation centre: Monitors the system and supports operational decisions.
1.6 Smart Grid Domains
The conceptual model shown in the lecture connects several domains. In simple words, these are the main areas that work together:
- Generation: Producing electricity.
- Transmission: Moving bulk electricity over long distances.
- Distribution: Delivering electricity through local networks to customers.
- Customer: Homes, buildings, businesses, and industrial users.
- Operations: Monitoring and controlling the grid.
- Markets: Activities relating to electricity buying, selling, and pricing.
- Service provider: Organizations that provide services and coordinate with users and grid operators.
Communication between these domains is important because information from one part of the grid can affect decisions in another.

The lecture identifies six important components.
1. Smart home
A smart home uses Smart Grid technologies to manage electricity consumption, respond to pricing information, and help the wider grid work efficiently.
2. Renewable energy
Renewable resources include solar, wind, hydropower, ocean energy, biomass, and geothermal energy. They can reduce dependence on non-renewable resources and may reduce pollution.
3. Consumer engagement
Consumers can take part through time-based pricing, net metering, appliance scheduling, and financial incentives.
4. Operation centre

1.8 Smart Home Technologies
Smart homes rely on three major elements: smart meters and home energy management systems, smart appliances, and home power generation.
A. Smart meters
A smart meter is a digital meter that measures electricity use and can exchange data between the consumer and the energy service provider.

Functions and benefits:
- Provides a digital interface between the consumer and provider.
- Supports automatic information exchange.
- Helps consumers understand their energy usage.
- Provides usage information to the electricity provider.
- Can support billing and demand-management processes.
B. Home Energy Management System (HEMS)
A Home Energy Management System helps consumers monitor and control electricity use in the home.

It can:
- Show energy use in more detail.
- Display real-time information and price signals.
- Schedule appliances to operate when electricity prices are lower.
- Help reduce peak demand.
- Support better balancing of electricity demand across an area.
Example: A washing machine can be scheduled to run late at night if the customer has a pricing plan that makes electricity cheaper at that time.
C. Smart appliances
Smart appliances can respond to signals or programmed settings. They may delay flexible operations during periods of high demand.
- They can automate some electricity-use decisions.
- They may respond to signals from the energy provider.
- The user can often override automated settings.
- Overriding the schedule can be convenient, but it may mean the consumer does not receive the lowest possible price.
D. Home power generation
A home may generate electricity using:
- Rooftop solar panels.
- Small wind turbines.
- Small hydropower systems where suitable.
- Home fuel-cell systems (described in the lecture as producing heat and power from natural gas).
Extra electricity may be fed back to the grid when the equipment and local arrangement permit it.

Islanding: In the lecture, islanding refers to a home or local system continuing to receive power from its own distributed resources when it is separated from the main grid. Whether this is safe or permitted depends on the system design and protection equipment.

Examples listed in the lecture:

- Wind
- Ocean energy
- Hydropower
- Biomass
- Geothermal energy
- Biofuels
- Hydrogen derived from renewable resources
Advantages in a Smart Grid

- Can reduce the consumer's dependence on the main grid.

- Helps integrate sustainable sources into the electricity network.
1.10 Consumer Engagement
Consumer engagement means allowing consumers to take an active role in managing electricity use and, in some cases, supplying electricity to the grid.

The lecture emphasizes three methods: Time-of-Use pricing, net metering, and financial incentives.
A. Time-of-Use (TOU) pricing
Under Time-of-Use pricing, electricity prices vary according to the time period. The lecture distinguishes:
- On-peak hours: Times when electricity demand is high.
- Off-peak hours: Times when electricity demand is lower.
Consumers may be encouraged to move flexible activities to off-peak times. This can reduce pressure on the grid and may lower their bills.

Example: A household schedules charging an electric vehicle for an off-peak period rather than a busy evening period.
B. Net metering
Net metering accounts for electricity taken from the grid and electricity sent back to the grid by a consumer, for example from rooftop solar panels.
- A smart meter can measure incoming and outgoing electricity.
- The final bill depends on the energy taken from the grid and the energy sent back.
- The value or payment for exported energy depends on the applicable arrangement and time-based rates.
- The lecture notes that consumers may receive incentives based on net metering values.
C. Financial incentives
An energy provider may offer incentives to encourage useful consumer behaviour, such as:
- Moving appliance use to off-peak hours.
- Using stored energy at a suitable time.
- Supplying energy to the grid from supported batteries or plug-in hybrid electric vehicles.
Why consumer engagement matters
Consumers may save money, while electricity providers can make electricity delivery and demand balancing more efficient.
2. Smart Grid – Part II
Main focus: Operation centres, distribution intelligence, electric vehicles, communication, security, and cloud applications.
2.1 Operation Centres
An operation centre monitors and controls the electricity transmission and distribution system.
Limitations of traditional operation centres
The lecture describes the following issues:
- They try to balance generated energy with the energy being used.
- They may have limited information about energy flow across the grid.
- Their control capabilities can be limited.
- They may not detect certain power oscillations early enough, which can contribute to a blackout.
Smart Grid operation centres
Smart Grid operation centres use more monitoring information and control capability. They can make the grid more reliable and help reduce the chance of widespread blackouts.
2.2 Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU)
A Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU) measures electrical quantities such as voltage and current at a particular location at a fixed sampling rate.
What a PMU does
- Samples voltage and current at its installed location.
- Gives a snapshot of the power-system condition at that location.
- Higher sampling can provide a more dynamic view of changing grid conditions.
- Helps operators identify possible problems or blackout risks earlier.
- Multiple PMUs can form a phasor network.
The data collected by a phasor network can be analysed by a central system such as SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition).
Easy example
Think of PMUs as high-speed monitoring points placed around the electrical network. Each point reports what is happening locally. Combining the reports helps operators understand the wider grid.
2.3 Self-Healing Grid and Demand-Side Distribution
Self-healing grid
A self-healing grid uses monitoring and control to respond to certain disturbances with less manual intervention.

The lecture lists actions such as:
- Damping unwanted power oscillations.
- Avoiding unwanted current flows.
- Rerouting power to avoid overloading a transmission line.
- Using distribution intelligence to help keep the grid operating.
Self-healing does not mean every fault is automatically fixed. It means the system can detect and respond to suitable problems using automated functions.
Demand-side energy distribution
Demand-side distribution considers the energy needs and consumption of customers. Supply, scheduling, and pricing can be managed with reference to demand and available resources.

The lecture also describes energy distributors forming a coalition to serve the energy needs of a specific geographic area.
2.4 Distribution Intelligence
Distribution intelligence means adding smart IoT devices and automation to the electricity distribution system.

With smart meters and other smart equipment, it can help:
- Identify the source of a power outage.
- Automatically change switching arrangements to maintain power flow.
- Balance real and reactive power more effectively.
- Protect and control feeder lines.
Real power and reactive power — simple understanding
- Real power is the power that performs useful work, such as running a motor or lighting a lamp.
- Reactive power is associated with energy temporarily stored and returned by electric or magnetic fields in equipment. It supports the operation of many AC devices but does not represent the same kind of net useful energy transfer as real power.
The lecture notes that reactive-power management helps control electrical current and maintain proper operation of the distribution system.
2.5 Plug-In Electric Vehicles (PEVs)
A Plug-In Electric Vehicle (PEV) is an electric vehicle that can be connected to an external electricity source to charge its battery.

The Smart Grid can help coordinate PEV charging.
Benefits and uses
- Can reduce dependence on oil.
- When running on electricity, the vehicle itself does not produce exhaust emissions at the point of use.
- The battery is charged using electricity from the grid.
- Charging during off-peak hours can reduce pressure during busy periods.
- In supported systems, a vehicle may send energy back to the grid during peak demand.
- The lecture mentions incentives for vehicles that provide energy to the grid by discharging.
2.6 Smart Grid Communication: Components
Smart Grid communication allows meters, home appliances, gateways, data aggregators, and control systems to exchange information.
Main components
1. Smart home appliances: Devices that use or control electricity.
2. Smart meters: Measure electricity use and exchange meter data.
3. Gateways: Connect a set of local meters/devices to another network.
4. Data Aggregator Units (DAUs): Combine information from meters in a particular area.
5. Meter Data Management Systems (MDMSs): Centrally manage meter data and support the communication system.
Networks used
- Home Area Network (HAN): Network within a home or building.
- Neighborhood Area Network (NAN): Network connecting meters/devices in a neighborhood.
- Wide Area Network (WAN): Network spanning a larger geographic area.
- IP networks: Networks that use Internet Protocol for communication.
- Sensor and Actuator Networks (SANETs): Networks of sensors that measure conditions and actuators that perform actions.

2.9 Smart Grid Security
A Smart Grid is a cyber-physical system because it combines physical electricity equipment with computing, communication, sensors, and control.
Main security concerns
- Integrity: Data must remain trustworthy and must not be changed improperly.
- Availability: Components and information should be available when required.
- Dynamic system attacks: An attacker may use knowledge of earlier information or system behaviour to construct a harmful request or signal.
- Physical threats: Attacks against physical equipment, such as a generator, substation, transmission line, or smart meter.
- Coordinated attacks: Several components or attack methods are targeted together, potentially causing failures to spread.
A. Data Injection Attacks (DIAs)
A data injection attack changes information such as sensor readings, control feedback, or electricity price signals.

Possible effects described in the lecture:
- A congested transmission line may appear safer than it really is because its measurements have been manipulated.
- Incorrect control decisions may be made.
- Lines may trip, generators may disconnect, load may need to be shed, or a blackout may occur.
B. Electricity-price manipulation
If an attacker changes price information, a customer or other party may be charged an incorrect amount or gain an improper financial benefit.
C. Time-synchronization attacks
PMUs rely on correct time references. If an attacker changes their time synchronization, the measurements may give a misleading picture of actual grid conditions. This can lead to incorrect control and protection actions.
D. Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks and availability
A Denial-of-Service (DoS) attack attempts to make a system, component, or information unavailable when needed.

In a Smart Grid, blocking important communication can reduce visibility of the grid's state and harm system stability. The lecture also notes the risk of manipulating the balance between generation and load.
E. Replay attacks
A replay attack occurs when an attacker records previous sensor outputs or messages and sends them again later, making old information appear current.

Simple example: A system receives an old “everything is normal” reading even though the real condition has changed.
F. Dynamic data injection attacks
A dynamic data injection attack uses knowledge of the grid's dynamic behaviour to inject data that can hide unstable conditions or make them difficult to observe. The lecture notes that this can contribute to system collapse.
G. Covert attacks
The lecture describes a covert attack as a closed-loop version of a replay attack.
H. Physical and coordinated attacks
- Physical attacks directly target equipment or may manipulate meters for electricity theft.
- Coordinated attacks exploit the links between grid components by targeting several parts or using multiple attack types at the same time.

Cloud computing can provide computing, storage, analysis, and security services for Smart Grid systems. The lecture groups Smart Grid cloud applications into three areas: energy management, information management, and security.
A. Energy management and cloud

- Incoming requests can be scheduled according to priority, available resources, and other constraints.
- Cloud computing can support microgrid management.
- Data from multiple smart meters can be analysed together.
- Real-time energy-use and pricing information can be shared.
- Mobile agents may be used to monitor systems across a heterogeneous Smart Grid.
B. Information management and cloud
Cloud computing supports storing, processing, and sharing information from different grid components.
- Real-time information can be managed across distributed systems.
- Parallel processing can help analyse large amounts of information.
- Cloud storage can hold information collected from smart meters.
- Cloud applications can support dynamic pricing and data access.
- Information from several consumers and energy sources can be combined for management decisions.
C. Security in the Smart Grid using cloud applications
The lecture describes cloud-based approaches for:
- Developing power-system information security and protection systems.
- Processing large volumes of user data using private-cloud resources.
- Helping utilities respond to malicious software.
- Analysing and estimating real-time data.
- Protecting privacy and supporting secure data storage and verification.
3. Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) – Part I
3.1 What Is the Internet of Things (IoT)?
The Internet of Things (IoT) aims to connect physical objects or “things” through communication technology. These objects can be uniquely identified and can exchange information.

Important ideas in the lecture:
- Connect smart things or objects globally.
- Give objects unique identities.
- Support interoperability, meaning that different objects and systems can work together.
3.2 What Is the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)?
The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) is the use of IoT technologies in industrial environments. It connects industrial machines, sensors, control systems, software, and business systems to improve industrial processes.

The lecture describes IIoT as linking automation systems with enterprise planning and product-lifecycle activities.
Simple example
A factory machine has sensors that measure temperature, vibration, and operating hours. These readings are sent to software that can help identify a possible problem before the machine stops working. This is an example of IIoT.
3.3 IIoT and Industry 4.0
The lecture connects IIoT with Industry 4.0, which is described through four industrial revolutions:
1. First Industrial Revolution: Mechanized production.
2. Second Industrial Revolution: Mass production.
3. Third Industrial Revolution: Electronics, the development of computing, and industrial automation.
4. Fourth Industrial Revolution: Connected and intelligent industry, commonly associated with IIoT, cyber-physical systems, and smart factories.
What is a smart factory?
A smart factory uses connected equipment, data, automation, and software to monitor and manage production more intelligently.
3.4 Main Technologies Used in IIoT
The lecture lists the following technologies and approaches:
- Machine learning: Software learns patterns from data and can support prediction or classification.
- Big data technology: Helps handle and analyse very large datasets.
- Machine-to-machine (M2M) interaction: Machines exchange information with each other.
- Automation: Machines or software carry out tasks with less manual intervention.
- Sensors: Collect real-world information from equipment and the environment.

- Physical objects.

5. Data Handling and Analytics – Part I
Main focus: Data handling, Big Data, cloud computing, data sources and flow, storage, and Hadoop.
5.1 What Is Data Handling?
Data handling is the process of collecting, organizing, storing, protecting, using, archiving, and disposing of data safely and appropriately.

The lecture explains that data handling includes policies and procedures for managing both electronic and non-electronic data during and after a project.
Why data handling is important in IoT
- IoT devices can generate a large amount of data.
- Sensors may send data continuously.
- Data must be stored and managed safely.
- Useful information must be extracted from the data.
- Poor-quality or unprotected data can lead to incorrect decisions or privacy problems.
5.2 What Is Big Data?
Big Data refers to datasets that are so large, fast, or varied that traditional data-processing methods may not be sufficient to store, manage, and analyse them effectively.

Big-data technology aims to capture, discover, process, and analyse large volumes of different data at high speed and extract useful value from it.
Why Big Data is connected to IoT
- Large numbers of IoT devices generate data.
- Sensors produce many readings over time.
- Data may arrive continuously and quickly.
- Different devices generate different types of data.
Example: A smart factory may collect temperature, vibration, power consumption, production, and maintenance data from hundreds or thousands of machines. The combined data may be too large or varied for a simple spreadsheet-based process.
5.3 Types of Data
A. Structured data
Structured data follows a predefined format or organization. It is often stored in rows and columns in relational databases.

Examples:
- Customer tables.
- Sales records with fixed fields.
- Electricity meter readings stored in a table.
SQL (Structured Query Language) is commonly used to manage and query relational data.

The lecture slides quote an approximate split of 20% structured data and 80% unstructured data. Treat this as a figure from the lecture material, not a universal or current measurement.
B. Unstructured data
Unstructured data does not follow one fixed table format.

Examples:
- Photos and images.
- Audio and video.
- Text documents and PDFs.
- Emails and social-media content.
- Sensor streams that do not follow a single simple database schema.
Traditional relational database systems may not be suitable on their own for every kind of unstructured data. Special storage and processing technologies can help analyse it.
5.4 The 7 Vs of Big Data
The lecture lists seven characteristics: Volume, Velocity, Variety, Variability, Veracity, Visualization, and Value.
1. Volume
Volume means the amount of data generated and stored.
- Data comes from more and more sources.
- IoT devices and sensors can create huge datasets.
- The lecture gives large image collections and video uploads as examples of high data volume.
Remember: Volume = how much data?
2. Velocity
Velocity means the speed at which data is generated, transmitted, and processed.
- Some data arrives continuously.
- Real-time services require fast processing.
- Older batch-based systems may struggle when data arrives too quickly.
The lecture includes historical examples such as social-media posts and financial-market data to illustrate high velocity.

Remember: Velocity = how fast does data arrive or need to be processed?
3. Variety
Variety means the different forms and sources of data.

The slides note that Big Data may be unstructured or semi-structured. Examples listed in the lecture include:
- Text.
- Images.
- Audio and video.
- Web data.
- GPS data.
- Sensor data.
- SMS messages.
- Documents and PDFs.
Remember: Variety = how many kinds of data?
4. Variability
Variability means data patterns, rates, or meanings can change. The meaning of information may depend on context.

For example, a particular word or hashtag may mean different things in different contexts. Sensor readings may also fluctuate over time.

Remember: Variability = how much do the data patterns or meanings change?
5. Veracity
Veracity concerns the trustworthiness and understandability of data.

Possible issues include:
- Bias.
- Noise.
- Incorrect or abnormal values.
- Incomplete or misleading readings.
Veracity matters because a system that makes decisions automatically can produce poor results when its input data is unreliable.

Remember: Veracity = can we trust and correctly understand the data?
6. Visualization
Visualization means presenting data in a visual form, such as charts, graphs, or diagrams.

It helps people:
- See patterns more easily.
- Compare values.
- Identify changes or unusual behaviour.
- Understand analytics and make decisions.
Remember: Visualization = how can data be shown clearly?
7. Value
Value means extracting useful information or benefits from data.

Collecting data alone is not enough. The data should help answer questions, solve problems, improve decisions, or create a practical benefit.

Remember: Value = what useful result can we get from the data?

5.9 Data Storage
A. File systems and distributed file systems
A file system organizes and stores files. A distributed file system stores data across multiple computers or nodes.

Distributed file systems aim to support properties such as:
- Consistency: Data should remain logically correct across the system.
- Availability: Data should be accessible when required.
- Fault tolerance: The system should continue operating despite certain failures.
The lecture mentions:
- Google File System (GFS).
- Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS).
- Kosmosfs.
The lecture notes that GFS was designed for large-scale files and that its performance may be limited for small files.
B. NoSQL databases
NoSQL databases are non-traditional relational database systems used for types of data or workloads that may be difficult to handle with only a conventional relational structure.

The lecture lists three main types:
1. Key-value databases: Store a value using a key that identifies it.
2. Column-oriented databases: Organize or store data around columns, which can help with certain large analytical workloads.
3. Document-oriented databases: Store records as documents, often with fields that do not all need to follow one rigid table structure.
Important: The lecture introduces NoSQL as a data-storage approach for Big Data; it does not mean that relational databases are never useful.
5.10 What Is Hadoop?
Apache Hadoop is an open-source software framework for distributed storage and processing of large datasets across a cluster of computers.

Instead of relying on a single machine, Hadoop can divide work and data across multiple machines. This helps scale storage and processing for large datasets.

The lecture says that Hadoop's HDFS and MapReduce were derived from ideas in Google's Google File System (GFS) and MapReduce.
5.11 Building Blocks of Hadoop
The lecture identifies four building blocks.
1. Hadoop Common
Contains shared utilities and supporting functions used by other Hadoop components.
2. Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS)
Stores data across multiple nodes and provides access to that data.
3. MapReduce
A programming and processing framework for processing large datasets in parallel across a cluster.

Simple idea: A large task can be broken into smaller tasks, processed across different machines, and then combined into a result.
4. Yet Another Resource Negotiator (YARN)
Manages cluster resources and helps allocate CPU, memory, and other resources to applications.

5.12 Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS)
HDFS is Hadoop's distributed storage system. The lecture describes a NameNode and multiple DataNodes.
NameNode
The NameNode stores filesystem metadata. Metadata is information about the files, such as which blocks belong to a file and where those blocks are stored.
DataNode
DataNodes store the actual data blocks.
Blocks and replication
- Large files are divided into blocks.
- The blocks are stored across DataNodes.
- Blocks can be replicated, meaning copies are kept on more than one node.
- Replication helps keep data available if a node fails.
NameNode vs DataNode
NameNode	DataNode
Manages filesystem metadata.	Stores the actual data blocks.
Tracks which blocks belong to files and where they are located.	Reports block information to the NameNode.
Coordinates filesystem operations.	Performs block storage, creation, deletion, and replication as instructed.


The lecture also mentions DataNodes communicating with each other for data rebalancing and replication, and verifying checksums before updating block information.
5.13 JobTracker and TaskTracker (As Presented in the Slides)
The lecture slides show the older Hadoop MapReduce management model using JobTracker and TaskTracker.
JobTracker
The slides describe the JobTracker as running with the NameNode and performing tasks such as:
- Receiving a job from the user.
- Deciding how many tasks or mappers should run.
- Deciding where each mapper should run.
TaskTracker
The slides describe a TaskTracker as running on each DataNode and performing tasks such as:
- Receiving a task from the JobTracker.
- Executing the task.
- Reporting progress back to the JobTracker.
The slides mention data locality: running work close to where the data is stored can reduce unnecessary data movement.
5.14 Hadoop Master-Slave Architecture
The lecture presents HDFS as a master-slave shared-nothing architecture.
Master role
The master (NameNode in the slide's HDFS explanation):
- Handles filesystem operations such as opening, closing, and renaming files and directories.
- Determines which DataNodes store the blocks of each file.
Slave role
DataNodes act as workers or storage nodes. They:
- Serve read and write requests from filesystem clients.
- Create, delete, and replicate blocks as instructed by the NameNode.
Simple example: The NameNode keeps the map showing where file blocks are located; DataNodes hold the blocks themselves.
